The origin of the name

WhyFrequentSuspicion?

FrequentSuspicion comes directly from PTSD—and from the suspiciousness of all things and all people that followed me home.

The battlefield trained my mind and body to search constantly for danger: to read a room before entering it, notice every exit, question every silence, measure every change in tone, study every motive, and assume that what looked safe might not be safe at all.

That vigilance helped me survive where danger was real. But the nervous system does not always understand when the war is over. The scanning continued. Suspicion became frequent because threat felt possible everywhere—in ordinary places, familiar voices, relationships, churches, kindness, and even peace itself.

“The battlefield exposed my heart. It didn’t write my story.”

The name does not celebrate distrust as wisdom. It names the condition honestly. It acknowledges the distance between discernment and fear, between genuine danger and a body that still expects it, and between surviving the past and learning how to live in the present.

FrequentSuspicion is the sound of bringing that hypervigilance before God—asking Him to separate truth from fear, conviction from shame, discernment from paranoia, and protection from isolation.

01

No edited testimony

I refuse to make myself the hero.

2009 The breach

I left the wife of my youth and the mother of my children, crossing into adultery without stopping to grieve what I had broken.

Sixteen years later The loop closes

Disability, coercive control, abuse, and a second divorce brought the unresolved grief of both marriages into the open.

The moral failure at the center of this testimony happened in 2009. I left the wife of my youth—the mother of my children—and crossed into adultery. I moved directly from one relationship into another without stopping to grieve my first marriage, reckon honestly with what I had done, or understand the foundation upon which I was building my new life.

I spent the next sixteen years trying to build a home on that foundation. But a life built without truth, repentance, and healing is a life built on sand. What appeared stable from the outside carried fractures that time alone could not repair.

As my health declined and I became disabled, the balance within my second marriage changed. In the place where vulnerability should have been met with compassion, I increasingly experienced coercive control and abuse. Eventually, the marriage collapsed under the weight of that control and the repeated threat of divorce—even when the immediate conflict seemed painfully small compared with the life we had built.

Naming what happened to me does not erase what I did in 2009. Being harmed does not make me innocent of the harm I caused. Both truths must be allowed to stand. I betrayed my first marriage, and I later experienced mistreatment within my second. Accountability does not require me to deny my own wounds, just as my wounds cannot be used to escape accountability.

This second divorce is closing a loop that remained open for sixteen years. I never truly grieved the wife of my youth, the family structure that was lost, the trust I broke, or the life that might have been. Now I am confronted with grieving all of it: the first marriage I abandoned, the second marriage that collapsed, the damage I caused, the harm I endured, and the years spent trying to build permanence on an unstable foundation.

FrequentSuspicion comes from inside that reckoning. These songs are not an attempt to make me the hero or the only victim. They are the sound of a man finally refusing to edit his own history—bringing his sin, trauma, anger, grief, and unanswered questions before God. The purpose is not to remain buried beneath the wreckage. It is to tell the truth, end the cycle, and learn how to build whatever remains on rock instead of sand.

02

Grace with a backbone

Mercy and accountability belong together.

These songs also confront what happens when churches, families, and respected leaders protect comfort or reputation instead of people. Forgiveness is not denial. Mercy is not passivity. Biblical love does not ask the wounded to call evil peace.

FrequentSuspicion challenges spiritual language used to conceal coercion, excuse cruelty, soften responsibility, or silence those who came forward with the truth. Grace heals because truth sees.

03

The purpose

Turn the wreckage into a warning—and a witness.

I cannot rewrite yesterday. I can tell the truth about it. If one veteran recognizes that surviving is not the same as healing, if one father turns toward his family, if one husband refuses the passing flame, or if one church chooses courage over image, then the ruins can become something other than a grave.

The music exists for veterans carrying invisible battles, broken families, questioning believers, people wounded by churches, and anyone wondering whether God still enters places filled with ashes.
